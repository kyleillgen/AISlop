# Build the beat around the player

The research supports building the flow engine around the player's own timing rather than around an external song. It also supports tuning tolerance and mercy invisibly instead of tuning challenge. Flow // Resonance already follows the best-supported pattern on most of its key choices. It detects the player's cadence in the 96–124 BPM band, where people synchronize most easily. It eases tempo toward that cadence, caps machine adaptivity by locking, and removes death. Its assistance only ever helps. The evidence calls for six changes. After lock, keep light phase correction (α ≈ 0.1–0.3) instead of a frozen grid. Keep a fixed "perfect" window that assistance never widens. Treat the 16th grid as a silent assist grid, never as a required rhythm. Fold latency and anticipation offset into a per-player calibration. Add explicit stopping cues so a 30 s–3 min burst ends cleanly. Hold rendering to a cached, timestamp-driven Canvas loop that sleeps when hidden. On music, Spotify is closed by policy as well as by technology: its Developer Policy says "Do not create a game" ([Spotify Developer Policy](https://developer.spotify.com/policy)). The better route is generated music, with local files or tap-tempo against whatever the user is already playing as options. One honest framing matters throughout. A burst this short can produce a measurable flow-like state, a mood and vigor lift and support for incubating a problem. It cannot reliably produce deep flow, and the evidence does not support claiming it makes anyone more productive.

## Three of nine flow conditions are designable, and "slightly too easy" is the safe side

**Strong evidence.** The flow literature separates conditions a designer controls (clear goals, immediate feedback, perceived challenge–skill balance) from experiences that follow (absorption, merging of action and awareness, time distortion). A meta-analysis of 28 studies found that **challenge–skill balance has only a moderate relationship to flow**. **Clear goals and sense of control predicted flow as strongly as balance did** ([Fong et al. summary](https://www.instituteofcoaching.org/resources/challenge%E2%80%93skill-balance-and-antecedents-flow-meta-analytic-investigation)). Imbalance is asymmetric. In a Beat Saber study with 201 players, difficulty matched to skill gave the most flow and too hard reduced it, but **too easy did not reduce flow** ([UvA record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/d9fed69c-e25f-442a-b635-c256755e4ec3)). A response-surface study of 528 players likewise found **flow higher when skill exceeds challenge than the reverse** ([Wojtasiński et al. 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-024-00846-4)). Removing death is defensible on the same grounds. Repeated deaths lower smiling and raise negative arousal ([van den Hoogen et al.](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235675672_Between_Challenge_and_Defeat_Repeated_Player-Death_and_Game_Enjoyment)), and flow follows an **inverted U against stress arousal and cortisol** ([Peifer et al. 2014](https://research.uni-luebeck.de/en/publications/the-relation-of-flow-experience-and-physiological-arousal-under-s/)). Failure still has a job to do: players judge a game better when they **feel responsible for their failures** ([Juul](https://jesperjuul.net/text/fearoffailing/)).

**What this means for timing.** Lab studies detect flow differences in self-report and skin conductance with **blocks as short as 30 s** ([Ulrich et al. 2016](https://academic.oup.com/scan/article/11/3/496/2375154)). A 2020 review of 38 studies warns, however, that deep flow may take about 25 minutes. It also found that many short-block studies assume flow from the difficulty condition rather than measuring it ([Khoshnoud et al. 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7751419/)). The defensible target is therefore "microflow": an absorbing ritual you can repeat, with all three preconditions met from the first second. It is not a promise of deep flow on every compile.

**The tension the design has to resolve.** The user also wants the game to help them think. Here the incubation evidence pulls the other way from deep absorption. Across 117 studies, incubation helps (mean d ≈ 0.29), and **high-demand filler tasks shrink the benefit**. For some insight problems, low-demand fillers beat rest ([Sio & Ormerod 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210055/)). In Baird et al., only the undemanding-task break improved creative performance. The gain appeared only on **problems already seen** and came with more mind-wandering ([Baird et al. 2012](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/schooler/jonathan/node/260)). So the engine should aim for fluent and low-demand, not maximally absorbing: hands busy, working memory free. A never-ramping, forgiving rhythm loop sits in exactly that spot. Escalating difficulty, puzzles or dense required patterns would undercut the "let them think" goal.

## Adapt to the player's cadence, then follow only lightly

**Strong evidence.** Adults' spontaneous tapping clusters at **500–600 ms per tap (100–120 BPM)**, and people synchronize best near their own rate ([Hammerschmidt et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.677201/full)). Music tempo databases peak around **120–125 BPM** ([Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.952488/pdf)). The 96–124 BPM band (484–625 ms) therefore sits on the human sweet spot. A large online sample complicates this: its mean was 780 ms, with clusters at roughly doubling intervals such as 525, 754 and 997 ms. Some players will therefore tap at half or double the band. Repp's review establishes three points ([Repp 2005](http://users.df.uba.ar/anita/f1_labo/clase1/repp%20psycho%20bull%20rev%202006%20synchro%20tapping%20review.pdf)):

- **Phase correction is fast and automatic**, even for shifts below perception.
- **Period (tempo) correction is slower** and depends partly on noticing the tempo change.
- Taps typically land **a few tens of ms ahead of the beat**, and a sound on each tap reduces that lead.

For the machine side, Fairhurst et al. found that a virtual partner correcting a moderate share of each asynchrony gave the best synchrony. An over-adaptive partner recruited executive-control areas ([Fairhurst et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22892422/)). Follow-up work found that **25–50% adaptivity improved synchrony, while 75–100% fell back to baseline or worse** ([Frontiers 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2022.916551/full)). Running studies give the clearest comparison of strategies (table below) ([PMC6283599](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6283599/)). Runners also entrain spontaneously only when the music is **within about 2.5% of their cadence** ([PMC4526248](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4526248/)).

| Alignment strategy (running, n = 36) | Synchrony R | Enjoyment effect |
|---|---|---|
| Tempo 20 BPM off cadence | 0.03 | — |
| One-time tempo match (fixed) | 0.65 | none |
| Continuous tempo matching | 0.80 | none |
| Continuous phase targeting (smooth oscillator) | 0.94 | **only strategy that raised enjoyment** |

**Inference for the engine.** "Detect, ease, lock" is right, but locking should cap *period* adaptation, not end all adaptation. After lock, keep a smooth phase correction of roughly α = 0.1–0.3 of each smoothed asynchrony. Never go near α = 1, which turns the beat into a moving target. Keep each period step below the threshold of notice: 1–2% of the beat interval per bar, never more than 2.5% in total from the player's detected cadence in one move. Larger steps force conscious period correction, which costs attention. Pleasure tracks how **easy the coupling feels**, not synchrony as such: felt groove is inversely related to how hard sensorimotor coupling is ([Janata et al. 2012](https://zenodo.org/record/997607)). That is the strongest single argument for adapting to the player rather than making the player adapt to a track.

## Tune tolerance and mercy invisibly; never touch the beat or the physics

**Measured evidence on hidden assistance is modest but consistent.** In one study a hidden adaptive timer **raised immersion** (IEQ 121 vs 114, p = .010). It also compressed the spread of scores: the score SD fell from 144 to 50 ([Denisova & Cairns 2015](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~pc530/pubs/Denisova_CHIPLAY15.pdf)). With 221 players, DDA improved performance but **not enjoyment or flow** ([Robb & Zhang](https://actaludologica.com/performance-based-dynamic-difficulty-adjustment-and-player-experience-in-a-2d-digital-game-a-controlled-experiment/)). DDA also produces **overconfidence** ([Constant & Levieux 2019](https://cedric.cnam.fr/index2.php/publis/article/CL19)), which is essentially the "I'm nailing it" feeling. Hiding the help matters. Assistance that is hard to recognise keeps credit with the player and raises fulfilment ([Nanno & Yanagisawa 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01934)). Even slight added cursor acceleration reduces the user's sense of agency ([UIST 2025](https://lab.plopes.org/published/2025-UIST-PrimedAction.pdf)). Commercial practice converges on forgiving timing and correcting outcomes, not changing physics. Celeste is the documented example: coyote time, jump buffering and corner correction ([Thorson](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/)). The documented mercy trick is a brief grace period at the moment of failure, such as BioShock's 1–2 s of invulnerability ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/tricks-of-the-trade-devs-reveal-how-their-games-fool-players)). Hamlet's design principle is to adjust gradually, and preferably for entities not yet on screen ([Hunicke & Chapman](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~hunicke/pubs/Hamlet.pdf)).

Typing research explains why the 16th-note quantizer is a good idea. Typists slowed after errors **only when correction was blocked**. When they could correct, they sped up ([Crump & Logan 2013](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/4683/Crump%20and%20Logan%20-%202013%20-%20Prevention%20and%20correction%20in%20post-error%20performanc.pdf)). Drawing attention to the hands also breaks fluent typing ([Logan & Crump 2009](https://www.crumplab.com/publications/Crump/files/3906/Logan%20and%20Crump%20-%202009%20-%20The%20left%20hand%20doesn't%20know%20what%20the%20right%20hand%20Is%20.pdf)). A quantizer that silently "lands" a slightly-off tap is an auto-correct, so it should keep the inner motor loop running. Loud per-tap judgments and combo-break popups work against it. Two further inferences follow, neither tested in games. Groove pleasure peaks at **medium syncopation** ([Witek et al. 2014](https://doaj.org/article/743d9013fbde436999b78a244c718d37)), so the music, not the obstacles, should supply rhythmic interest. And because the caudate responds in *anticipation* of a musical payoff ([Salimpoor et al. 2011](https://brams.org/2011/01/09/salimpoor/)), telegraphing phrase resolutions a bar ahead gives the player something to ride toward.

The parameter recommendations below combine sourced anchors with design inference. Each row says which is which.

| Parameter | Recommendation | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence band | 96–124 BPM. Fold detected intervals of 750–1250 ms or 250–310 ms into the band by doubling or halving | SMT clusters (strong); folding rule (inference) |
| Cadence detection | Median inter-tap interval over 6–8 taps. Lock when the CV of inter-tap intervals is ≤ 5% and tempo is within 2.5% of the detected cadence | 2.5% from the running study (strong); CV threshold untested (inference) |
| Tempo steps | ≤ 1–2% per bar, applied at bar boundaries | Repp; running studies (strong); exact step (inference) |
| Post-lock phase correction | α 0.1–0.3 on the smoothed asynchrony | Fairhurst (strong for α 0.25–0.5); lower end (inference) |
| Re-sync trigger | Rolling 8-tap tempo drifts > 4% for ≥ 2 bars, or a pause > 1.8 s | 1.8 s anticipation limit (strong); 4% (inference) |
| Required-action spacing | ≥ 2 beats per obstacle (≈ 1 s), never faster than 8ths | 150–200 ms tapping cap, 100–125 ms threshold (strong) |
| Quantize grid | 16ths at most, as silent assist only | ±62 ms snap sits below what players perceive as asynchrony (inference from strong data) |
| Perfect window | Fixed ±35–45 ms after offset correction. Assist never widens it | osu! OD5 300-band = ±50 ms ([osu! wiki](https://osu.ppy.sh/wiki/Overall_difficulty)); fixed policy (inference) |
| Assist target | 85–95% clean-pass rate over the last 16–24 obstacles, with hysteresis | 85% rule is about learning rate, not enjoyment ([Wilson et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6831579)); band (inference) |
| Assist rates | Rise within 1–2 clips; decay over 8–16 clean bars; floor = baseline | Hamlet gradualism (dev); asymmetry from Beat Saber result (inference) |
| Where assist acts | Gaps of obstacles not yet visible; collision tolerance; post-clip grace | Hamlet "proactive"; Celeste (dev) |
| Never adapt | Gravity, flap impulse, scroll speed mid-arc, audio–visual offset, visible obstacles | Agency evidence (UIST, aim-assist guidance) (inference) |

## External songs fail on policy, latency and effort

The question of syncing to Spotify has a clear answer, and the answer is no. Since **27 Nov 2024**, new apps cannot call Audio Features or Audio Analysis, so they get no beats, bars or tempo ([Spotify blog](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2024-11-27-changes-to-the-web-api)). Development-mode apps are limited to **5 users** and require the owner to have Premium ([Feb 2026 migration guide](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-api/tutorials/february-2026-migration-guide)). Decisively, the Developer Policy prohibits games. It also prohibits syncing recordings to visual media, overlapping Spotify Content with other audio, and analyzing the content ([Spotify Developer Policy](https://developer.spotify.com/policy)). The Web Playback SDK plays through DRM (EME) and exposes no raw audio ([SDK reference](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-playback-sdk/reference)). YouTube bans separating or modifying the audio of its content ([YouTube policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). Apple says MusicKit's playback time is deliberately not observable at high rate ([Apple forums](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/687487)).

Even if the APIs were open, the entrainment evidence favours the adaptive design. A fixed song helps only when its tempo falls within about 2.5% of the player's cadence. Otherwise the player has to rely on effortful period correction, which no amount of window widening removes. The recommended music architecture has three tiers:

1. **Default: generated, in-key music.** It is scheduled on `AudioContext.currentTime` with a 25 ms look-ahead tick and a 100 ms scheduling window ([web.dev, two clocks](https://web.dev/articles/audio-scheduling)). This gives sample-accurate sync, unlimited tempo control and no licensing risk.
2. **Optional: "use my file."** Decode a dropped MP3 once and estimate BPM and offset offline with web-audio-beat-detector `guess()` ([README](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/web-audio-beat-detector@8.2.37/README.md)). Then time-stretch the track to the player's cadence and treat it like generated music.
3. **Optional: "tap along to anything."** The player taps to music playing in another app and the engine locks on through its existing cadence detector. This needs no API and raises no policy issue.

To serve "feels like I'm nailing it", tune assistance, not the song.

## Flow // Resonance against the evidence

The reference game gets the architecture right, and most of the changes are refinements. It keeps the beat in the natural-cadence band and eases toward the player instead of imposing a tempo. It caps adaptivity, and it now gates lock on actually reaching the player's tempo, which matches the Fairhurst and running findings. It spaces required actions about one second apart, far from motor limits. It quantizes silently, which is the typing auto-correct principle. It never kills the player: a clip costs streak, which keeps failure attributable to the player without the stress spike of death, and the shield is BioShock-style mercy. It never ramps difficulty and deepens through music and visuals instead, so it stays in the "skills ≥ challenge" region the evidence favours. Its auto-glide mode, which forgives two pillars on return, is the documented "first shots miss" pattern. It also means looking away costs nothing. The assist controller acts only to help and decays back to baseline, never below it. The +26 px ceiling on a 175 px gap (about 15%) is modest enough to stay unnoticed if applied correctly.

| Area | Keep / change | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Lock behaviour | **Change** | Replace the frozen grid with α 0.1–0.3 phase correction. Make re-sync a sequence of sub-threshold period steps, never a jump |
| Cadence detection | **Change** | Fold half- and double-time tappers (≈ 50–62 or ≈ 192–248 BPM) into the 96–124 band rather than forcing them |
| Grid | **Keep 16ths for snapping; require 8ths or coarser** | At 124 BPM a 16th is 121 ms, beyond comfortable one-finger tapping and at the perceptual sync threshold ([Repp 2006](https://doaj.org/article/dc3f29b3977e4adf8dfe9aa998eb40de)) |
| Perfect window | **Fix it** | ±35–45 ms around the player's calibrated mean, never widened by assist. Assist widens only survival margins, which protects felt achievement if the help is ever discovered |
| Calibration | **Add** | Use the cadence phase to also measure the mean tap–beat asynchrony. That single number captures anticipation (taps landing early) plus device latency. Keep tap sounds off during this phase ([Rhythm Quest devlog 10](https://rhythmquestgame.com/devlog/10.html)). Draw at `currentTime − outputLatency − userOffset` ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioContext/outputLatency)), re-read on device change, and expose a manual offset |
| Guide-line pull | **Restrict** | Apply it as a correction to the outcome near a gap, not as a force felt through the whole arc. Never let it push against a tap |
| Gap widening | **Restrict** | Change only obstacles not yet on screen. A gap that "breathes" while visible exposes the help |
| Feedback | **Change** | Mute per-tap judgment text. Celebrate at phrase or bar boundaries. Give each tap its own sound |
| Stopping cues | **Add** | Resolve the music at loop ends (for example 8–16 bar cadences) and wind tempo down over the final bars. End on a build-done event when available. Avoid cliffhangers |
| Milestones | **Reconsider** | 500-point epochs and Platinum must not create "protect the streak" pressure. That is attention residue carried back into work ([Leroy, via UW Bothell](https://www.uwb.edu/news/2024/05/17/an-ongoing-study-on-the-success-of-staying-focused)). Persist progress across bursts so quitting mid-epoch costs nothing |
| Rendering | **Audit** | Bake gradients and glows into offscreen canvases. Avoid `shadowBlur` and per-frame text ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Canvas_API/Tutorial/Optimizing_canvas)). Cap DPR at 2, keep a fixed particle pool, use `alpha:false`. On `visibilitychange`, suspend the AudioContext, because audible pages are exempt from timer throttling ([Chrome](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/timer-throttling-in-chrome-88)). Drive motion from the audio clock |

The measurement plan should rely on telemetry, because DDA inflates players' own skill estimates. The stride signals to log are:

- the rolling coefficient of variation of inter-tap intervals, normalized because timing variability scales with interval length ([Repp 2005](http://users.df.uba.ar/anita/f1_labo/clase1/repp%20psycho%20bull%20rev%202006%20synchro%20tapping%20review.pdf));
- the mean and SD of pre-quantization asynchrony;
- burst length between pauses longer than 1.8 s;
- whether the next 2–4 intervals lengthen after a clip.

None of these has been validated as a flow measure. They are proxies for fluency, so thresholds must be tuned per player. The success metrics are:

- an occasional (1-in-N) two-item check after a burst, taken from the Flow Short Scale's fluency and absorption subscales ([FKS manual](https://www.millisecond.com/library/v7/flow/flowshortscale/flowshortscale/flowshortscale.manual));
- a "how long did you play?" estimate compared with actual time, as a time-distortion proxy;
- how often players keep going past the end cue. A high rate is good for engagement and a warning sign for overrun.

## Conclusion

The core insight is that the engine's job is to make coupling feel effortless, not to manage challenge. In a one-button game with no death and no ramp, almost every lever for "I'm nailing it" is about time. Meet the player's tempo, follow their phase gently, absorb their errors silently and pay off the musical phrase they are riding toward. Challenge-balance machinery matters less than the flow literature's popular image implies. Control, clear goals and low-latency feedback carry as much weight, and erring easy costs little. That reframes the assist controller as a secondary safety net beneath a timing engine that does most of the work.

The unresolved risk lies not in the mechanics but in the goal. Deep absorption and "letting the mind think" pull in opposite directions, and the incubation evidence favours the lighter state. The game should therefore be judged on whether players return to work refreshed, with a loaded problem a little further along, rather than on how long they stay in. Two cheap studies would settle the open questions: a within-player A/B of frozen grid vs α ≈ 0.2 phase following, and of streak milestones on vs off, each measured on the fluency items, the duration estimate and how quickly players resume work. None of the specific thresholds here (CV ≤ 5%, the 85–95% band, the ±40 ms perfect window) has been validated in a rhythm micro-game, so treat them as starting points for tuning, not as findings.
